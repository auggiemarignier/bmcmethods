# Linear Gaussians

## Sum of Transformations of Multivariate Gaussians

Suppose that some observed data $d\in\mathbb{R}^M$ are generated by
several independent physical processes

$$d = \sum_{j=1}^{J} A_j X_j,$$

where

- $X_j \in \mathbb{R}^{N_j}$ is the amplitude of the $j$-th physical
  process,

- $A_j \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times N_j}$ is the linear mapping relating the
  process to the observations.

We suppose that each $X_j$ is assigned a Gaussian prior
$\mathcal{N}(\mu_j, \Sigma_j)$ expressing our beliefs about the process
prior to observing the data.

Here we prove the unoriginal result that the sum of linear
transformations of independent Gaussian random variables is also
Gaussian.

The characteristic function of a multivariate Gaussian random variable
is

$$\begin{aligned}
    \varphi_X(t) & = E\left[e^{it^\top X}\right]                         \\
                 & = \int e^{it^\top x}f_X(x) \mathrm d x                \\
                 & = \exp\left[it^\top\mu - \frac12t^\top\Sigma t\right]
\end{aligned}$$

Since the $X_j$ are independent, their joint density
factorises as

$$p(X_1,\dots,X_J)=\prod_{j=1}^J p(X_j)
    =
    \prod_{j=1}^J
    \mathcal{N}(X_j;\mu_j,\Sigma_j).$$

For each $j$, the linear transformation $A_j X_j$ is itself Gaussian, with

$$A_j X_j \sim \mathcal{N}(A_j\mu_j, A_j\Sigma_j A_j^\top).$$

Because the $X_j$ are independent, the transformed variables $A_j X_j$ are also independent since each transformation depends only on its own variable.
The characteristic function of $d$ therefore factorises:

$$\varphi_d(t) = \prod_{j=1}^J \varphi_{A_j X_j}(t).$$

Using the Gaussian characteristic function,

$$\varphi_{A_j X_j}(t)
    =
    \exp\left(
    i t^\top A_j\mu_j
    -
    \frac12 t^\top A_j\Sigma_j A_j^\top t
    \right),$$

we obtain

$$\varphi_d(t)
    =
    \exp\left(
    i t^\top \sum_{j=1}^J A_j\mu_j
    -
    \frac12 t^\top \left(\sum_{j=1}^J A_j\Sigma_j A_j^\top\right)t
    \right).$$

This is still the characteristic function of a multivariate Gaussian random variable.
Therefore the observed data are Gaussian:

$$d \sim \mathcal{N}\left(
    \sum_{j=1}^J A_j\mu_j,
    \sum_{j=1}^J A_j\Sigma_j A_j^\top
    \right).$$

Equivalently, the density of $d$ is

$$p(d)
    =
    \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{M/2}
    \left|\sum_{j=1}^J A_j\Sigma_j A_j^\top\right|^{1/2}}
    \exp\left[
        -\frac12
        \left(
        d-\sum_{j=1}^J A_j\mu_j
        \right)^\top
        \left(\sum_{j=1}^J A_j\Sigma_j A_j^\top\right)^{-1}
        \left(
        d-\sum_{j=1}^J A_j\mu_j
        \right)
        \right]$$

provided the covariance matrix is invertible.
If it is singular, then $d$ is still Gaussian, but the distribution is degenerate and has no density with respect to the Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^M$, although it possesses a density on its supporting affine subspace.

This result is the basis for the linear-Gaussian inverse problem: once some of the latent processes are treated as unknowns and the remainder are marginalised out, the unmodelled contribution simply appears as an effective Gaussian term in the likelihood.

## Inference

Suppose we now want to infer some of the latent variables. Since the
ordering of their indices is arbitrary, without loss of generality, we
take the set $\{X_1,\dots,X_k\}$ to be those that we infer and
$\{X_{k+1},\dots,X_J\}$ to be nuisance parameters that are marginalised
out. The forward model can be separated as

$$d = \sum_{j=1}^k A_j X_j + \eta,$$

defining $\eta$ to be the aggregate
nuisance contribution

$$\eta := \sum_{m=k+1}^J A_m X_m$$

Again, since the $X_m$ are independent, the sum of their transformations is also a Gaussian

$$\eta \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\sum_{m=k+1}^J A_m \mu_m, \sum_{m=k+1}^J A_m \Sigma_m A_m^\top\right) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\eta, \Sigma_\eta)$$

If we collect the inferred variables into a single vector we have

$$X_\I = \begin{pmatrix}
        X_1    \\
        \vdots \\
        X_k
    \end{pmatrix}
    \quad
    \mu_\I = \begin{pmatrix}
        \mu_1  \\
        \vdots \\
        \mu_k
    \end{pmatrix}
    \quad
    \Sigma_\I = \mathrm{diag}\left(\Sigma_1,\dots,\Sigma_k\right)
    \quad
    A_\I = \left(A_1\;\dots\;A_k\right)$$

where the block diagonal covariance follows from the independence of the inferred variables. The prior is then

$$p(X_\I) = \mathcal{N}\left(X_\I; \mu_\I, \Sigma_\I\right).$$

Then the forward model is

$$d = A_\I X_\I + \eta,$$

which is the usual Gaussian linear inference problem, with the likelihood

$$p(d | X_\I) = \mathcal{N}(d; A_\I X_\I + \mu_\eta, \Sigma_\eta).$$

The posterior is obtained by the usual solution after applying Bayes' theorem

$$\begin{aligned}
    p(X_{\I}\mid d)
     & \propto p(d\mid X_{\I})p(X_{\I})                                                                           \\
     & \propto \exp\left[-\frac12 (d-A_{\I}X_{\I}-\mu_\eta)^\top \Sigma_\eta^{-1}(d-A_{\I}X_{\I}-\mu_\eta)\right]
    \exp\left[-\frac12 (X_{\I}-\mu_{\I})^\top \Sigma_{\I}^{-1}(X_{\I}-\mu_{\I})\right]                            \\
     & \propto \exp\left\{-\frac12 \left[(d-A_{\I}X_{\I}-\mu_\eta)^\top \Sigma_\eta^{-1}(d-A_{\I}X_{\I}-\mu_\eta)
    +(X_{\I}-\mu_{\I})^\top \Sigma_{\I}^{-1}(X_{\I}-\mu_{\I})\right]\right\}                                      \\
     & \propto \exp\left[-\frac12 Q(X_{\I})\right].
\end{aligned}$$

Expanding the quadratic form gives

$$\begin{aligned}
    Q(X_{\I})
     & =
    (d-\mu_\eta)^\top \Sigma_\eta^{-1}(d-\mu_\eta)
    -2X_{\I}^\top A_{\I}^\top \Sigma_\eta^{-1}(d-\mu_\eta)
    +X_{\I}^\top A_{\I}^\top \Sigma_\eta^{-1}A_{\I}X_{\I} \\
     & \quad
    +X_{\I}^\top \Sigma_{\I}^{-1}X_{\I}
    -2X_{\I}^\top \Sigma_{\I}^{-1}\mu_{\I}
    +\mu_{\I}^\top \Sigma_{\I}^{-1}\mu_{\I}.
\end{aligned}$$

Collecting the terms that depend on $X_{\I}$,

$$\begin{aligned}
    Q(X_{\I})
     & =
    X_{\I}^\top \Lambda X_{\I}
    -2X_{\I}^\top h
    +c,
\end{aligned}$$

where

$$\begin{aligned}
    \Lambda & = \Sigma_{\I}^{-1}+A_{\I}^\top \Sigma_\eta^{-1}A_{\I},                                   \\
    h       & = \Sigma_{\I}^{-1}\mu_{\I}+A_{\I}^\top \Sigma_\eta^{-1}(d-\mu_\eta),                     \\
    c       & = (d-\mu_\eta)^\top \Sigma_\eta^{-1}(d-\mu_\eta)+\mu_{\I}^\top \Sigma_{\I}^{-1}\mu_{\I}.
\end{aligned}$$

Completing the square gives

$$\begin{aligned}
    Q(X_{\I})
     & =
    (X_{\I}-\Lambda^{-1}h)^\top \Lambda (X_{\I}-\Lambda^{-1}h)
    +c-h^\top \Lambda^{-1}h.
\end{aligned}$$

Therefore

$$p(X_{\I}\mid d) \propto \exp\left[-\frac12 (X_{\I}-\Lambda^{-1}h)^\top \Lambda (X_{\I}-\Lambda^{-1}h)\right],$$

so the posterior is Gaussian,

$$p(X_{\I}\mid d)=\mathcal N(X_{\I};\mu_{\mathrm{post}},\Sigma_{\mathrm{post}}),$$

with

$$\Sigma_{\mathrm{post}}=\Lambda^{-1} = \left(\Sigma_{\I}^{-1}+A_{\I}^\top \Sigma_\eta^{-1}A_{\I}\right)^{-1},$$

and

$$\mu_{\mathrm{post}}
    =
    \Lambda^{-1}h
    =
    \Sigma_{\mathrm{post}}
    \left(
    \Sigma_{\I}^{-1}\mu_{\I}
    +
    A_{\I}^\top \Sigma_\eta^{-1}(d-\mu_\eta)
    \right).$$

## Model Comparison

If we wish to compare different modelling choices, for example different
selections of nuisance parameters or different forward operators, we
require the marginal likelihood (or evidence)

$$p(d)=\int p(d\mid X_{\mathcal I})p(X_{\mathcal I})\,\mathrm dX_{\mathcal I},$$

which normalises the product of the likelihood and prior.

Earlier we showed that the sum of linear transformations of independent Gaussian random variables is itself Gaussian.
Since

$$d=A_{\mathcal I}X_{\mathcal I}+\eta,$$

where $X_{\mathcal I}\sim\mathcal N(\mu_{\mathcal I},\Sigma_{\mathcal I})$ and $\eta\sim\mathcal N(\mu_\eta,\Sigma_\eta),$ are independent, it follows immediately that

$$d
    \sim
    \mathcal N\!\left(
    A_{\mathcal I}\mu_{\mathcal I}+\mu_\eta,\,
    A_{\mathcal I}\Sigma_{\mathcal I}A_{\mathcal I}^\top+\Sigma_\eta
    \right).$$

Therefore the evidence is

$$p(d)
    =
    \frac{
    1
    }{
    (2\pi)^{M/2}
    \left|
    A_{\mathcal I}\Sigma_{\mathcal I}A_{\mathcal I}^\top+\Sigma_\eta
    \right|^{1/2}
    }
    \exp\!\left[
        -\frac12
        (d-A_{\mathcal I}\mu_{\mathcal I}-\mu_\eta)^\top
        \left(
        A_{\mathcal I}\Sigma_{\mathcal I}A_{\mathcal I}^\top+\Sigma_\eta
        \right)^{-1}
        (d-A_{\mathcal I}\mu_{\mathcal I}-\mu_\eta)
        \right]$$
